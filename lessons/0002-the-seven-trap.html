The "7" Trap

A double threat your opponent can't block — because the blocks are stacked on top of each other.

Warm-up (30 seconds, from Lesson 1)

Before the new idea, prove the old one is still there. No peeking at Lesson 1.

The idea: stacked threats

Lesson 1's open three put two threats side by side. A defender who sees them can at least pick one. The "7" trap is nastier: it puts two threat squares in the same column, one directly above the other — and you own both. Now blocking isn't just insufficient, it's suicidal: the block disc becomes the floor your upper threat lands on. (Concept: Wargamer, "How to win Connect 4"; the stacked-threat mechanism is why Allis's solution tracks threats by the square they need, not the discs already placed — Allis 1988.)

The formation is five discs drawing a "7": a horizontal three and a diagonal three sharing their corner disc. Here it is for Red — the corner is d3, shared by the row (b3–c3–d3) and the diagonal (b1–c2–d3):

Count Red's ways to win: the row can complete at a3 or e3; the diagonal completes at e4. Look at e3 and e4 (marked): stacked. If Yellow ever blocks e3, that disc is the platform for Red's e4. Column e belongs to Red forever — and a3 is a spare. It doesn't matter which way the 7 faces; mirrored and flipped versions all work. (Wargamer)

Puzzle 1 — Build the 7

You are Red. Four of the five discs are placed; the bar of the 7 is missing one disc. Click the column that completes the formation. (Note: it won't create an immediate threat — you're building structure whose threats come due later. That's what makes it invisible to opponents who only look one move ahead.)

Puzzle 2 — Spring the trap

The 7 is built, and Yellow has just played e1 — a disc in your poisoned column. You can now force a win in two moves. Which column starts the kill?

Puzzle 3 — Defending (or coaching) against it

Flip perspectives — this is the one to remember when you're letting your kid win, or teaching them to defend.

Take it to the living room

The 7 takes about five quiet moves to assemble, and nobody who only checks for three-in-a-rows will see it coming — the completing move in Puzzle 1 looks completely harmless. Build it low and off-center while "trading" harmless moves. And for the graceful-loss games: assemble a 7, then conspicuously don't spring it — see if your kid ever notices the poisoned column. When they finally do, they've independently discovered stacked threats, which is genuinely deep Connect 4.

Theory teaser: "Column e is Red's forever" is your first taste of the endgame logic that solved the game: wins in Connect 4 are claims on future squares, and whoever holds the right claims can simply wait while the board fills. Why the height of the claimed square (odd or even row) decides everything is the next theory lesson — odd/even threats.
