Connect 4 Strategy · Lesson 4 · ~9 minutes

Zugzwang

Your banked claim doesn't win the game. The other player's forced move does.

Warm-up (20 seconds, from Lesson 3)

The idea: run them out of safe moves

Lesson 3 left one thing unexplained. You've banked an odd claim — a square that's yours if someone fills the column up to it. But nobody's going to volunteer. Why would Yellow ever play under your winning square?

Because in Connect 4, you must move. There is no pass. Every turn, each player burns one square off the board — and when all the harmless squares are gone, whoever's turn it is must play a harmful one. That's zugzwang: the obligation to move becomes the losing weapon, and the endgame is nothing but an arithmetic race — who runs out of safe squares first? (Allis devotes a chapter of the thesis to exactly this control-of-zugzwang accounting; see also MIT's slides.)

So the skill is counting. Call a square neutral if either player can fill it without consequence. On your turn with the claims banked: if the number of neutral squares is odd, you take the last one and your opponent cracks first. If it's even, you're the one who'll be squeezed — you need to change the count (or make a new threat) before entering the endgame. This is the parity of Lesson 3 wearing work clothes.

Play it — the squeeze

Below is a real endgame. You are Red, and your position is exactly what this course has taught you to build: a banked odd claim at a3 (your b3–c3–d3 three, with a1 already filled — so a2 is lava for Yellow). Three neutral squares remain: e6, f6, g6. Three is odd, and it's your move. Squeeze.

Now play it again and lose it. Reset, and this time play column a yourself. Watch Yellow calmly take a3, and feel the game go dead in your hands — every disc you drop afterward is just filling a drawn board. (Now and then Yellow will even cash the leftovers on the top row — row 6 is even, and Lesson 3 told you whose row that is.) One careless tempo is the difference between a won endgame and nothing.

Check — the count

Take it to the living room

Real games against your kid now have a script: build quietly (Lesson 2), bank one odd claim (Lesson 3), then stop attacking. Count the neutral squares, keep the count in your favor, and make cheerful harmless moves until they run out of road — the win will look like an accident. And the graceful-loss lever is the same tool reversed: when you hold a winning claim but want a draw, spend one move in your own claim's column at the right moment. The claim dies invisibly — no thrown game, no suspicious blunder, just "wow, you defended really well today."

The full circle: you now hold the actual shape of the 1988 solution — center first, build double and stacked threats, bank claims on your parity, and win the zugzwang count. Allis's proof is these four lessons made rigorous: nine rules that certify, from move one, that the first player can always keep a claim the zugzwang arithmetic will eventually cash. (Allis, A Knowledge-based Approach of Connect-Four)
