The Double Threat

One threat gets blocked. Two threats win the game.

You already block threats when you see them — and so does your kid, most of the time. The games you lose (or fail to win) aren't lost to a threat you missed. They're lost to a position where two threats appeared at once, and blocking one just handed over the other.

Almost every won game of Connect 4 ends this way. Strong play isn't about sneaking a four past someone — it's about deliberately manufacturing a double threat: two ways to win, both live on the same turn. Your opponent gets one block. You win with the other. (papergames.io advanced guide)

The one rule of this lesson

The simplest double threat is the open three: three in a row with both end squares empty and playable (a square is playable if a disc dropped there lands on it right now). Two live ends, one block available — game over.

So the skill has two sides:

Attacking: grow your pairs so they can become open threes.
Defending: kill open threes at the pair stage — once both ends are live, no block saves you.

Take it to the living room

This week, in games with your kid: don't try to win fast. Just build a pair on the bottom row with empty space on both sides, and watch whether they defend the ends. If they only ever block completed threes, you now win at will — and when you'd rather not win, "fail" to notice their pairs and let them find the open three themselves. The delighted shriek when a kid first discovers a double threat is the whole point.

Why this is also the deep theory: Victor Allis's 1988 proof that Connect 4 is a first-player win is, at heart, an accounting of threats — which player can bank a threat that must eventually come due. Double threats are the entry-level version of that idea; odd/even threats (a future lesson) are the grandmaster version. (Allis, A Knowledge-based Approach of Connect-Four, 1988)
