Warm-up (15 seconds, from Lesson 8)

The idea: certificates, not moves

Everything in this lesson comes straight from chapter 6 of the thesis — I extracted the definitions from the source rather than paraphrasing folklore, and the full table lives on the new nine-rules card. (Allis, A Knowledge-based Approach of Connect-Four, 1988, ch. 6–7)

Here's the reframe that makes the thesis readable. Allis doesn't prove positions by searching moves — he proves them with certificates. Each rule is a small, local guarantee of the form: "the player who is content to wait can be sure of getting certain squares." A guaranteed square refutes every enemy four-window that needs it. Show that a compatible set of certificates refutes every enemy window, and the position is mathematically decided — no search, no lines, no "and then if they play here."

You already own the two most important certificates without knowing their names. Claimeven — answer on top, collect the even square — is Lesson 3's shadowing. Vertical is why towers are noise. The others are variations on three motifs: you can't play two squares in one turn (Baseinverse; Baseclaim, its hybrid with Claimeven), odd + odd = even (Lowinverse, Highinverse — Lesson 4's counting, weaponized), and the elegant one you haven't met: the Before family — "for every empty square of my own group, I either take it or instantly take the square above it; either I win or they can never collect all the successor squares." Aftereven is its all-even special case, a win that doubles as a barricade.

The origin story

The nine weren't handed down from mathematics. Allis's §6.10 is candid: the four elementary rules came from playing experience; then his program VICTOR kept failing to prove positions he could see were safe, and each failure was debugged into a new rule — Aftereven, then Before ("a major improvement", also the most expensive to compute), then Highinverse, then the last two, whose impact he rates "probably not significant." Then the failures stopped, and he stopped: "no search for extra rules was made." The most famous result in the game's history is, structurally, a debugging log — nine rules because nine was enough.

Take it to the living room

This thread is for you, not the kids — but it does produce one practical gift: you can now read the thesis's endgame diagrams and certify a drawn position at the board, the way you certify a won one. When you tell your kid "this game is a draw now, want to see why?", you can lay Claimevens across the board like receipts. That party trick is the entire 1988 proof, performed live.
