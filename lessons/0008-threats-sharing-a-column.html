Threats That Share a Column

The basement rule: when claims stack up in one column, only the lowest one is real.

Warm-up (45 seconds, from Lesson 1)

Long time since Lesson 1 — prove it survived. Fresh position, same law.

The idea: the basement rules the building

You know two column situations already: you own both stacked squares (Lesson 2 — poisoned column, you win) and claims in separate columns (Lesson 3 — parity decides). Lesson 8 is the missing case: your claim and theirs in the same column.

The law is one sentence: discs enter a column from the bottom, so the lowest claim gets decided first — and everything above it is hostage. The opponent of the basement-claim's owner can never fill toward it (the square below it is poisoned for them, Lesson 6 style), so the upper claim simply never comes due. Two consequences that override earlier rules of thumb:

Hostage beats parity. A wrong-parity claim in the basement still rules the column — and a beautiful odd claim sitting above an enemy claim is worth nothing. So your claim-evaluation checklist gains its final ordering: ① is the window alive? ② is it lowest in its column? ③ only then, parity. (This is the informal skeleton of the threat-interaction analysis in Allis's thesis; the full formal version is his nine strategic rules.)

Check 2 — The endgame audit

Why no clever tricks here: you might hope to "spend" a hostage column to flip the zugzwang count (Lesson 4). Run the arithmetic for this position: entering the column triggers a forced three-move sequence (your fill, their block, your counter-block) — three squares consumed, turn passed — but it also exposes three fresh neutral squares above. Two parity flips cancel; the count stands. Real tempo surgery in Connect 4 is exactly this finicky, which is why Allis needed nine interlocking rules where this course gives you rules of thumb. If the theory itch strikes, the thesis's threats chapter is the scratch.

Take it to the living room

Practical habit: whenever you spot a claim — yours or theirs — trace straight down its column before you celebrate or panic. A kid's scary-looking high threat above your low one needs no answer at all (save the tempo; Lesson 6, question 5). And your stage-3 kid who's started banking claims is ready to be shown this the discovery way: let them build a proud high claim over your quiet low one, play the squeeze out, and afterward rebuild just the column: "which square was going to get filled first?"
