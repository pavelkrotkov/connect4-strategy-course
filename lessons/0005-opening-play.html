Opening Play

Why the first move is d — and the three replies you'll actually see across the kitchen table.

Every evaluation in this lesson ("wins", "draws", "loses") is perfect-play truth, queried directly from Pascal Pons' perfect solver — not strategy-guide folklore. You can paste any line into that site and check me.

Warm-up (20 seconds, from Lesson 4)

Why d, precisely

Two reasons, one shallow and one deep. The shallow one is geometry: the board has exactly 69 four-in-a-row windows, and 51 of them touch column d — versus 39 for c/e, 27 for b/f, and a mere 15 for an edge column. The two most connected squares on the whole board are d3 and d4, sitting in 13 windows each. (Counted exhaustively by script for this course; you can verify with pencil and patience.)

The deep reason is Lesson 3's: to win, Red must bank an odd claim, and claims need windows. Starting at d1 grabs the square with the most windows on the bottom (odd!) row and is the only start from which Red can force such a claim against best defense. The solver's verdict on all seven first moves:

d → Red wins · c, e → draw · b, f → Red loses · a, g → Red loses fastest

Yes: with perfect play on both sides, opening on the edge doesn't just waste the advantage — it hands the win to the second player. (gamesolver.org; first proved by Allis, 1988.)

And after 1. d1? Every Yellow reply loses. The most stubborn defense is d2 (it loses slowest); the edges a1/g1 lose fastest. Your job is knowing how to punish the replies you'll actually meet.

Drill 1 — They copy you (best defense)

Your kid plays d2 right on top of you. Careful: this is the one position in this lesson where only one move keeps the win — the solver scores every alternative as a loss for Red.

Drill 2 — The snuggle (c1)

The most common casual reply: a disc next to yours. The punish is genuinely non-obvious — and the "natural" center stack is a trap.

Drill 3 — The corner kid (a1)

Small opponents love corners. Several moves win here, but one is fastest — and one very plausible-looking move actually loses.

Capstone — play a full game

Everything, from move one. Yellow here plays like an attentive kid: it takes any win, blocks any three you complete, and otherwise fills safely — so single threats won't beat it. Open at d, build structure (Lesson 2), bank the right parity (Lesson 3), and convert (Lessons 1 and 4). It plays differently every reset. One honest note: this Yellow only sees squares that win or lose this turn — it cannot read a banked claim. Neither can most kids. That blindness is exactly why banking works.

Take it to the living room

Memorize exactly three sentences: copy-me gets d3; a snuggle gets the far flank (c1→f1, e1→b1); a corner gets the center stack (d2 — and never play beside their corner). Past move three, stop remembering and start playing the course: structure, parity, count. For the graceful losses you now have the earliest lever of all — open at c or e instead of d, and the game is an honest draw from move one; nobody can tell you gave anything away.
