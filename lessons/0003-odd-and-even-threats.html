Odd and Even Threats

Why the first player wins a perfect game — and which of your claims are real money.

Warm-up (45 seconds, from Lesson 2)

Lesson 2's trap — but this one faces the other way, so pattern-matching won't save you; you have to see the mechanism. The 7 is built and Yellow's position is riddled. Force the win in two moves.

The idea: claims come due by row parity

Number the rows from the floor: row 1 at the bottom, row 6 on top. Rows 1, 3, 5 are odd; rows 2, 4, 6 are even.

Now the key scene. Late in a close game, both players are just filling space, each avoiding the other's threat squares. Here's the trick available to Yellow (the second player): whenever Red starts a fresh column, Yellow can simply answer on top of Red's disc, again and again. Play that out in your head: Red's disc lands on row 1 — odd. Yellow's lands on row 2 — even. Red row 3, Yellow row 4. In every column where Yellow "shadows" like this, Red collects the odd squares and Yellow collects the even squares. (This "follow-up" idea and the threat-parity rules are formalized in Allis's thesis; a compact version is in MIT's Connect Four slides.)

So when the dust settles, a lasting claim (a threat square you own that isn't playable yet) is only money if it sits on a row your color will actually receive:

Red — the first player — banks odd claims (rows 1, 3, 5).
Yellow — the second player — banks even claims (rows 2, 4, 6).

A Red claim on an even row usually evaporates: in the slow fill-out, Yellow gets that square for free. This single idea is the spine of the 1988 solution: opening in the center is the only first move that lets Red force an odd claim against best defense — which is why center-first wins and everything else doesn't. (Allis 1988; Allis interviewed in Reader's Digest.)

Fine print: these are the headline rules. Allis's full rule set handles the messy cases — multiple claims, claims sharing a column (your stacked pair from Lesson 2 is one such special case: it wins regardless of parity). And note verticals never bank at all: a vertical three's completing square is playable immediately, so it's always block-on-sight — which is why your kid's beloved tower attack never beats an attentive opponent.

Check 2 — Reread the 7 with new eyes

Lesson 2's position again. Red's three claims are marked: a3, e3 (the row's two ends) and e4 (the diagonal). You know the stacked pair e3/e4 wins on the spot. But suppose the stack didn't exist — which claim would still win the endgame for Red all by itself?

Take it to the living room

From now on, every time you form a quiet three, count its empty square's row from the floor. On 1, 3, or 5: sit on it, trade moves, let the board fill — the square is yours. On 2, 4, or 6: treat it as a bluff, useful only to scare an opponent into wasting a block. And this is your subtlest handicap lever yet for the graceful losses: play "strong-looking" moves that only bank even claims. You'll look dangerous, threaten loudly, and quietly never actually win — no suspicious blunders required.
