The Trap Zoo

The 7 is not a trap. It's one animal in a genus — and you're about to recognize the whole family.

Warm-up (15 seconds, from Lesson 2)

The idea: one anatomy, many bodies

Everything this course calls a trap has the same skeleton: two of your lines whose empty finishing squares sit stacked in one column. Blocking the lower square floors the upper one (Lesson 2); owning both makes the stack parity-proof (Lesson 8). The 7 — a row plus a rising diagonal — is just the easiest body to build around that skeleton. Any two lines that can finish one-above-the-other work: two diagonals, a diagonal over a row, a diagonal coming down from above. Recognition is shape-specific, so today is a gallery visit: four new bodies, same skeleton. (Every position below was constructed for this course and machine-verified — legal, no accidental threats, claims exactly as labeled. Mechanism: Allis 1988, threats chapter.)

Exhibit 1 — The Rails

Two parallel diagonals, one row apart — the fat diagonal band that kids (and adults) build half by accident. Each rail finishes in the same column, one square above the other. Red has just built this; you're coaching Yellow. Which column is lava?

Exhibit 2 — The Hook

The 7 builds its stack from below — bar plus a diagonal rising to the column. The hook builds the same stack from above: a bar plus a diagonal descending onto the column from the right. Study it — no drill, just read the skeleton:

The bar (b3–c3–d3) claims a3 and e3; the descending diagonal (d5–f3–g2, finishing at e4) stacks e4 on top of e3. Same poisoned column e as Lesson 2's diagram — built from the opposite direction. And look closely at the marks on the left: this build quietly created a second stack, a3/a4, via the down-diagonal d1–c2–b3. Two poisoned columns from one quiet shape.

Exhibit 3 — The X

Two diagonals crossing: one rising to d4, one descending to d5. The stack is loaded and Yellow can't see it. Force the win in two.

Exhibit 4 — The Mirage

The expert's real skill isn't spotting traps — it's rejecting fakes. Here Red has two menacing diagonals again, rising from the left and descending from the right. Look carefully at where each one finishes.

The universal test

Don't memorize the gallery. Run the skeleton check on any scary shape, yours or theirs: trace each three-disc line to its empty finishing square. Two squares, same column, stacked → real trap, treat per Lessons 2 and 8. Two lines pointing at one square → one claim wearing a costume; a single block (or the wrong parity) kills it. Two squares in different columns → not a trap at all, just claims — audit them: alive, lowest, parity (Lesson 8).

Take it to the living room

Two field notes. First: the rails are the trap your kid will build by accident — kids love laying discs along a diagonal wall. When it happens, don't dodge it silently; lose to it once, honestly, then replay the column ("where did both your lines finish?") — that's the discovery version of this whole lesson. Second: your own scan (Lesson 6, question 4) just got an upgrade — "what are they building?" now means trace their lines to the finish squares, not "does it look like a 7?"
