Playing Second

Half of all games start with the disadvantage. Here is what the second seat is actually playing for.

As in Lesson 5, every "wins / draws / loses" below is perfect-play truth queried from the solver — position strings given so you can check.

Warm-up (15 seconds, from Lesson 9)

The idea: three different games

The second seat plays one of three games, decided by Red's very first disc:

Game 1 — Red takes d1 (a strong opponent). With perfect play you lose; your job is maximum resistance. Answer d2 (loses slowest, −1 vs −2 elsewhere — Lesson 6), then play for the draw with the tool you met in Lesson 9: Claimeven — answer on top, collect even squares. Your whole economy runs on Lesson 3 flipped: your money rows are 2, 4, 6. Bank even claims, kill Red's odd ones early (they never expire on their own), and wait for the error. Against humans, it comes.

Game 2 — Red takes c1 or e1 (a decent opponent). The game is an honest draw, and it's yours to hold. After 1. c1, four replies keep the balance — c2, d1, e1, f1 — while a1, b1, g1 lose (solver "3"). Better: holding correctly sets a tripwire field. After 1. c1 d1, only two of Red's seven follow-ups keep Red's draw (c2 and d2) — the other five lose the game for Red on the spot (solver "34"). The second seat doesn't beg for a draw; it sells one at extortionate prices.

Game 3 — Red plays anything else (most kitchen tables). You are winning, by force — if you punish precisely. And here the center dogma breaks: after 1. a1 you win with b1 or d1 (+2 each), but after 1. b1 the only winning reply is c1 — taking the center d1 merely draws (solver "1", "2"). The punish is position-specific; that's what the drills below burn in. (Mirrors apply: g1 ↦ f1/d1, f1 ↦ e1.)

Take it to the living room

This lesson is for the day your kid learned Lesson 5 — from your own fridge, probably — and grabs d1 with a grin. That day you've succeeded, and your game changes seats: d2, even claims, tripwires, patience. Notice the beautiful symmetry with the coaching ladder: an opponent who opens d1 against you is stage 4. Losing the first seat is the diploma. (And when you'd rather they win their d1 games while they're learning: skip the tripwires — hold the draw passively and let their initiative tell. That's the second seat's version of the graceful loss.)
