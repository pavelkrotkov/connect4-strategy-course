Allis's Nine Strategic Rules

The certificates that solved the game. Each rule guarantees "the controller of the zugzwang" (the player content to wait — usually the defender) certain squares, thereby refuting enemy groups. Cover every enemy group with compatible certificates and the position is decided. Extracted from Allis (1988), ch. 6.

The four elementary observations

1 · Claimeven
Needs: two stacked empty squares, upper one even. Guarantee: the controller takes the even square, by answering on top when the odd one is played. Refutes: every enemy group using that even square. Course echo: Lesson 3's shadowing — this is it, formalized.
2 · Baseinverse
Needs: two directly playable squares. Guarantee: the opponent can't take both in one turn, so the controller gets at least one. Refutes: only groups needing both squares.
3 · Vertical
Needs: two stacked empty squares, upper one odd. Guarantee: at least one of the two. Refutes: groups needing both (necessarily vertical ones). Course echo: why towers are noise (Lesson 3).
4 · Lowinverse
Needs: two columns, each with two stacked empties, both uppers odd. Guarantee: odd + odd = even total, so the opponent enters first; the controller gets at least one of the two odd uppers (plus Vertical side-effects). Refutes: groups needing both uppers. Course echo: Lesson 4's neutral-square counting.

The two workhorses (born from VICTOR's failures)

5 · Aftereven
Needs: a group of your own completable using only the even squares of Claimevens (the "Aftereven group"). Guarantee: you will eventually complete it — and in whichever column finishes it, the opponent never gets a square above it. Refutes: enemy groups needing a square above the Aftereven group in every column where the group still needs a disc (already-filled columns don't count). A win that doubles as a barricade.
6 · Before
Needs: a group of yours with no enemy discs, whose empty squares avoid the top row. Guarantee: for each empty square — take it, or instantly take the square above it when the opponent plays it. Either you complete the group (game over) or the opponent can never collect all the successor squares. Refutes: enemy groups needing all the successors. Allis: costliest rule to compute, biggest jump in strength.

The long tail

7 · Highinverse
Lowinverse extended one square up: two columns × three stacked empties, uppers even. Guarantees a square from the uppers-pair and from the middles-pair, plus each column's own top-two vertical, plus cross-effects when a bottom square is playable. Added when Lowinverses caused trouble just above their own squares.
8 · Baseclaim
Three playable squares + an even square above the second: an adaptive certificate that becomes a Baseinverse or a Claimeven depending on the opponent's choice. Refutes two families of groups at once.
9 · Specialbefore
A Before whose group includes a directly playable square, paired with another playable square in a different column. Its Before-style refutation narrows: enemy groups must need all the successors and the extra playable square; in exchange it adds Baseinverse-style coverage of the two playables. Added last alongside Baseclaim; Allis rates their impact "probably not significant."

The origin story (§6.10): the nine weren't derived — they were debugged. The four elementary rules came from player experience; every failure of VICTOR (Allis's program) on a position he could see was safe produced a new rule — Aftereven, then Before, then the tail — until the failures stopped. "No search for extra rules was made." The list is nine because nine was enough.

Compatibility (ch. 7): certificates can't overlap on squares with conflicting promises — the thesis's second half is the bookkeeping of which instances may be used together. This is the machinery behind every "it's finicky" in this course.
