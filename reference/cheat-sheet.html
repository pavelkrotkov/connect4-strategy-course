The Field Guide

Everything from Lessons 1–3 on one printable page. Terms link to the glossary.

Before the game

1 · Take the center. Moving first, play column d — it's the only opening that wins against perfect defense, because every horizontal window on a row passes through it. Second player: vs d1 answer d2 — but the center is NOT always the punish (vs b1 only c1 wins; d1 just draws): see the openings card's second-player table. (Allis 1988; Lesson 11)

Attacking

2 · Win with two threats, not one. A single threat gets blocked. Manufacture a double threat — the simplest is the open three: three in a row, both ends empty and playable. (Lesson 1)

3 · Stack threats when you can. Two of your threat squares in one column, one above the other, poison the whole column: any block floors your upper win. The "7" formation (horizontal three + diagonal three sharing a corner disc) builds a stack in five quiet moves; so do its cousins — parallel diagonals (rails), a descending diagonal over a bar (hook), crossed diagonals (X) — see the trap zoo. Spring any of them by filling the column to just below your lower square. Beware the mirage: two lines finishing on ONE shared square are a single claim, not a stack. (Lesson 2, Lesson 10)

4 · Bank claims on YOUR rows. Count a claim's row from the floor. First player: odd rows (1, 3, 5) are money — sit on them and let the board fill. Even-row claims are bluffs. Second player: exactly reversed. (Lesson 3)

Defending

5 · Block pairs, not threes. An open three is already a loss — the real block happens when the opponent has TWO with both sides open. (Lesson 1)

6 · A threat is only urgent if its square is playable. Empty column below it = latent. But watch latent threats' rows: an opponent claim on their parity row must be defused early or never. (L1, L3)

7 · Map the poisoned columns. Before "just filling," check: does my disc here floor an opponent threat above? Stacked opponent claims make a column lava — route around it and force them to enter it first. (Lesson 2)

8 · Verticals are noise. A vertical three's completion square is always immediately playable — block on sight and it's dead. Towers never beat attention. (Lesson 3)

The endgame

9 · Win the count, not the fight. Once claims are banked, stop attacking and count the neutral squares (safe for both players). Odd count on your turn: take neutrals and let the move-obligation crush them. Even count: you're the one being squeezed — burn a tempo or make a fresh threat before the board locks. (Lesson 4)

10 · The basement rules the building. Claims sharing a column: only the lowest is real — everything above it is hostage, whatever its parity. Audit every claim in order: window alive? lowest in its column? right parity? A claim failing any check counts for nothing in the endgame arithmetic. (Lesson 8)

Every move: the five-question scan

11 · Scan before you drop. ① Can I win right now? ② Can they? ③ Does my disc floor a square they want (look one square above your landing spot)? ④ What are they building (open pairs, a 7 in progress)? ⑤ Is their threat even real — window dead? claim on the wrong parity? Blocking dead threats wastes the tempi that decide endgames — and when two blocks kill one window, take the one on the better square. (Lesson 6)

Playing the kids

To win quietly: build low, off-center structure (the 7 looks like nothing); bank one odd claim; trade harmless moves and wait.

To lose gracefully: threaten loudly on the wrong parity — even-row claims look scary and never pay out. Leave your own pairs unextended, "miss" their open pairs, and let them find the double threat. Holding a won endgame you'd rather not win? Quietly spend one move in your own claim's column — the claim dies with no visible blunder.

To coach: when they lose to a trap, replay it and ask "which column was lava?" — discovering stacked threats themselves beats being told.
